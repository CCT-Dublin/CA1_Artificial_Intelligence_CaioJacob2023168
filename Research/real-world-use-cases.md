# Real-World Use Cases of Computer Vision

## 1. Introduction

Computer Vision is used in different real-world situations, from analysing images to identifying people. Looking at specific cases helps to understand how the technology affects individuals and organisations in practice.

This document examines three possible case studies involving facial recognition. The first concerns a wrongful arrest in Detroit, the second examines the use of live facial recognition by the Metropolitan Police in London, and the third concerns the collection and use of facial images by Clearview AI.

The aim is to compare the applications, their potential benefits and their documented ethical, legal and social concerns before selecting a case for the final poster.

## 2. Case Study One: Robert Williams and the Detroit Police Department

### 2.1 Background

In January 2020, Robert Williams was wrongly arrested by the Detroit Police Department after facial recognition technology produced an incorrect result during an investigation into a shoplifting incident. Investigators used a low-quality image from store surveillance footage to search for a possible match.

Williams was not the person shown in the footage. However, the facial recognition result contributed to the investigation that led to his arrest (University of Michigan Law School, 2024).

The case later became the subject of a lawsuit against the Detroit Police Department. In June 2024, the parties reached a settlement that introduced restrictions and additional safeguards for the police use of facial recognition technology (University of Michigan Law School, 2024).

### 2.2 Benefits and intended use

Facial recognition can help investigators identify possible leads when they have an image of an unknown person. It can potentially reduce the time required to search large image collections.

However, a possible match is not proof that a person committed a crime. The Detroit case illustrates the importance of distinguishing between an investigative lead and reliable evidence.

### 2.3 Ethical and social impacts

The case raises concerns about accuracy, fairness and accountability. An incorrect match can affect an innocent person's freedom, reputation and family life.

It also raises questions about the responsibility of police officers when they rely on automated results. The technology did not make the arrest decision by itself; the result was used as part of a human investigation.

The case is particularly relevant to discussions about racial fairness. The University of Michigan Law School reported that Williams was one of three known people wrongly arrested in Detroit after police relied on facial recognition results, and all three were Black (University of Michigan Law School, 2024).

### 2.4 Measures introduced after the case

The 2024 settlement introduced several safeguards. These included prohibiting arrests based solely on facial recognition results or photo line-ups directly following a facial recognition search. Police must obtain independent and reliable evidence linking a suspect to a crime.

The settlement also requires training about the technology's risks and an audit of cases dating back to 2017 in which facial recognition was used to obtain an arrest warrant (University of Michigan Law School, 2024).

These measures provide a useful example of how a real-world incident can lead to changes in policy and oversight.

### 2.5 Why this case may be suitable

This case has a clear sequence of events, identifiable consequences and documented changes introduced after the incident. It could support a discussion of accuracy, bias, human responsibility and practical ways to reduce harm.

One limitation is that the case concerns policing in the United States. If selected, the poster would need to distinguish the legal framework applicable in the United States from the data protection and AI regulations relevant in the European Union.

## 3. Case Study Two: Live Facial Recognition by the Metropolitan Police in London

### 3.1 Background

The Metropolitan Police Service in London uses facial recognition in several policing contexts. Its live facial recognition system compares images of people passing through a designated camera area with a police watchlist (Metropolitan Police, n.d.).

The police state that facial recognition can support crime prevention, the identification of wanted individuals and the protection of vulnerable people. The technology is used as an aid to policing rather than as a general-purpose identification system for every situation (Metropolitan Police, n.d.).

### 3.2 Potential benefits

Live facial recognition may help officers identify people on a watchlist in a specific location. This can provide an additional investigative tool when used for a defined policing purpose.

The Metropolitan Police has also commissioned testing of facial recognition performance in operational settings. Its published information discusses testing for possible differences associated with characteristics such as race, age and sex (Metropolitan Police, n.d.).

### 3.3 Ethical and social concerns

The use of live facial recognition in public places raises questions about privacy and surveillance. People passing through a camera area may have their images processed even if they are not suspected of an offence.

The technology also raises concerns about false matches, watchlist selection and the consequences of a person being incorrectly flagged. Its impact depends on factors such as the system's settings, operational procedures and how officers respond to a possible match.

A further issue is public trust. People may disagree about whether the potential benefits for crime prevention justify the processing of their facial images in public spaces.

### 3.4 Safeguards and oversight

The Metropolitan Police publishes information about its facial recognition policies, data protection assessments, equality impact assessments and deployment records. Its description of retrospective facial recognition also states that a human operator reviews a suggested match and decides whether it is considered a match (Metropolitan Police, n.d.).

These procedures provide material for examining transparency and human oversight. However, the existence of policies and assessments does not, by itself, establish that every deployment is free from errors or other negative impacts.

### 3.5 Why this case may be suitable

This case is useful for examining the balance between public safety and privacy. It also provides official documentation about the operation of the technology and the safeguards used by a police force.

A limitation is that the police's own publications mainly describe their procedures and stated objectives. A balanced assessment would also need independent evidence about outcomes, accuracy and the experiences of people affected by deployments.

## 4. Case Study Three: Clearview AI and the Collection of Facial Images

### 4.1 Background

Clearview AI provides a facial recognition service that can search a database of facial images. The Dutch Data Protection Authority investigated the company's processing of personal and biometric data.

In a decision finalised on 16 May 2024, the Dutch authority found that Clearview had processed personal data and biometric data relating to people in the Netherlands without the required legal basis. It also found problems concerning transparency and individuals' ability to exercise their data access rights.

The European Data Protection Board reported that the Dutch authority imposed a fine of €30.5 million and orders intended to end the identified violations (European Data Protection Board, 2024).

### 4.2 Intended use and potential benefits

Facial recognition databases can allow users to search for images that resemble a face submitted to the system. In a law enforcement context, this capability may be used to generate investigative leads.

The potential benefit is the ability to search a large collection of images more quickly than would be possible through a fully manual search. However, the usefulness of a result depends on the quality of the system and the legitimacy of the data and purpose involved.

### 4.3 Ethical, legal and social impacts

The case raises questions about whether people should have their facial images collected and used for identification without their knowledge.

Facial images and biometric information can be sensitive because they relate to a person's identity. Large-scale collection also raises concerns about transparency, data protection and whether individuals can exercise their rights.

The Dutch authority's decision demonstrates that these concerns can have legal consequences. Its findings included violations relating to the lawfulness of processing, biometric data, transparency and access requests under the General Data Protection Regulation (European Data Protection Board, 2024).

### 4.4 Possible safeguards

Organisations developing or using facial recognition services should establish an appropriate legal basis for processing personal data and comply with the applicable rules for biometric information.

They should also provide the information required by data protection law, make it possible for individuals to exercise their rights and review whether the collection and retention of facial images are necessary for the intended purpose.

This case also shows why compliance should be considered during the design and operation of a system, rather than only after a regulator investigates it.

### 4.5 Why this case may be suitable

The Clearview AI case is particularly relevant to privacy, biometric data, transparency and legal compliance. It has a documented regulatory decision and a clear connection to European data protection law.

One limitation is that the case focuses heavily on the collection and processing of facial data. The final analysis would need to explain the connection between these practices and the wider social effects of facial recognition.

## 5. Comparison of the Three Cases

| Case | Main issue | Potential benefit | Main concern | Evidence available |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Robert Williams, Detroit | Incorrect facial recognition result used in a police investigation | Generating investigative leads | Wrongful arrest, fairness and accountability | Documented incident and 2024 settlement |
| Metropolitan Police, London | Live facial recognition in public spaces | Supporting police identification and crime prevention | Privacy, surveillance and false matches | Official policies, assessments and deployment records |
| Clearview AI, Netherlands | Collection and processing of facial images for recognition | Searching large image collections | Biometric privacy, transparency and legal compliance | Regulatory decision and financial penalty |

The three cases demonstrate different aspects of facial recognition. The Detroit case focuses on the consequences of an incorrect result, the London case raises questions about public surveillance, and the Clearview AI case highlights the collection and legal processing of biometric data.

The evidence also needs to be interpreted carefully. A documented incident or regulatory decision can support specific conclusions, but it should not be treated as proof that every facial recognition system has the same problems or produces the same outcomes.
