# Benefits and Mitigation of Facial Recognition

## 1. Introduction

Facial recognition is a Computer Vision application that can provide useful support in areas such as criminal investigations, identity verification and security. However, the benefits of the technology depend on how accurately it performs and how responsibly organisations use its results.

The Robert Williams case demonstrates that an incorrect facial recognition result can have serious consequences when it is not properly verified. Therefore, increasing the positive impacts of facial recognition requires organisations to reduce technical, ethical, legal and social risks.

## 2. Potential Benefits

### 2.1 Supporting Criminal Investigations

Facial recognition can help investigators search large collections of images and identify possible matches more quickly than a completely manual process.

In a policing context, a facial recognition result can provide an investigative lead that officers can investigate further. This can potentially reduce the time required to search large numbers of images.

However, the result should remain an investigative lead rather than being treated as proof of identity.

### 2.2 Supporting Identification

Facial recognition can also be used to compare a person's facial image with an existing database for identification or verification purposes.

When used in an appropriate context, this can make identification processes faster and provide an additional tool for organisations.

The benefit depends on the accuracy of the system, the quality of the input images and the procedures used to interpret the results.

### 2.3 Improving Investigative Efficiency

One potential advantage of Computer Vision is the ability to process large amounts of visual information quickly.

Instead of manually comparing every image in a large collection, an automated system can identify possible candidates for further investigation.

This does not eliminate the need for human judgement. Instead, the technology can be used to reduce the amount of manual searching required.

## 3. Main Risks Identified in the Robert Williams Case

The Robert Williams case demonstrates several risks associated with facial recognition.

### 3.1 False Identification

The most important risk was the incorrect identification of Williams as a possible match.

A false match can have serious consequences if the result is treated as reliable evidence without independent verification.

### 3.2 Poor-Quality Images

The surveillance image used in the investigation was of poor quality.

This demonstrates that image quality is an important factor when interpreting facial recognition results. Organisations should consider whether an image is suitable for reliable comparison before relying on the output.

### 3.3 Over-Reliance on Automated Results

The case also demonstrates the danger of relying too heavily on an AI-generated result.

The technology should support human decision-making rather than replace proper investigation and independent evidence.

### 3.4 Demographic Performance Differences

NIST research has demonstrated that demographic differences can occur in the performance of facial recognition algorithms.

The size and direction of these differences vary depending on the algorithm, task and other conditions. Therefore, organisations should evaluate the specific systems they use rather than assuming that all facial recognition systems have identical performance (Grother, Ngan and Hanaoka, 2019).

## 4. Technical Mitigation

### 4.1 Test System Performance

Facial recognition systems should be tested before being deployed in situations where errors could seriously affect individuals.

Testing should consider different image qualities, operational conditions and relevant demographic groups.

Regular testing is also important because performance should not be assumed to remain appropriate in every context.

### 4.2 Assess Image Quality

Organisations should establish procedures for determining whether an image is suitable for facial recognition.

Poor-quality, blurred or poorly illuminated images should be treated with additional caution.

If an image does not provide sufficient information for reliable comparison, the result should not be treated as strong evidence.

### 4.3 Monitor False Positives

False positive rates should be monitored because an incorrect match can have serious consequences.

Organisations should record and review errors to identify patterns and determine whether changes to the system or its operational procedures are required.

### 4.4 Evaluate Demographic Performance

Systems should be evaluated across relevant demographic groups.

NIST research demonstrates why demographic performance should be measured rather than assumed. Testing can help organisations identify differences in error rates and determine whether additional safeguards are necessary.

## 5. Human and Operational Mitigation

### 5.1 Human Oversight

Human oversight should be an essential part of facial recognition use.

An operator should review a potential match and consider the available evidence rather than automatically accepting the system's recommendation.

However, human oversight should be meaningful. Staff need appropriate training to understand the limitations of facial recognition and the possibility of false matches.

### 5.2 Independent Verification

Facial recognition results should be independently verified before decisions with serious consequences are made.

In the Robert Williams case, the 2024 settlement introduced restrictions preventing arrests based solely on facial recognition results or photo line-ups directly following a facial recognition search (University of Michigan Law School, 2024).

This provides a practical example of how independent evidence can be required before an AI-supported investigation leads to serious action.

### 5.3 Staff Training

Employees using facial recognition should receive training about:

- false positives and false negatives;
- image-quality limitations;
- demographic performance differences;
- appropriate interpretation of AI results;
- the importance of independent evidence;
- their responsibilities when using AI-supported systems.

Training can reduce the risk of users treating an AI result as more reliable than it actually is.

## 6. Legal and Regulatory Mitigation

### 6.1 Clear Policies

Organisations should establish clear policies defining when facial recognition can be used and what actions can be taken following a potential match.

The policies should identify responsibilities and establish safeguards for decisions that could significantly affect individuals.

### 6.2 Auditing

Regular audits can help organisations identify inappropriate uses, errors and weaknesses in operational procedures.

The 2024 Detroit settlement included an audit of cases dating back to 2017 in which facial recognition was used to obtain an arrest warrant (University of Michigan Law School, 2024).

This demonstrates how retrospective review can be used to identify previous cases that may require further examination.

### 6.3 Transparency and Accountability

Organisations should document how facial recognition systems are used and how significant decisions are made.

Clear records can make it easier to investigate errors and establish responsibility when problems occur.

Transparency can also help build public understanding and trust in the use of AI.

### 6.4 Data Protection

Where facial recognition involves personal and biometric information, organisations must consider applicable data protection requirements.

The GDPR provides specific protection for biometric data used for the purpose of uniquely identifying a person under Article 9 (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2016).

The legal requirements depend on the jurisdiction and the specific use of the technology.

## 7. Increasing the Positive Impacts

The positive impact of facial recognition can be increased by using the technology for clearly defined and legitimate purposes.

Organisations should focus on situations where the technology provides a genuine benefit and where the risks can be appropriately controlled.

The technology should support human decision-making rather than replace it. Using facial recognition to generate investigative leads, followed by independent verification, can allow organisations to benefit from faster image searches while reducing the risk of serious errors.

Regular evaluation can also help organisations identify whether the technology is actually achieving its intended purpose.

## 8. Reducing the Negative Impacts

The negative impacts can be reduced through a combination of technical, operational and regulatory safeguards.

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| False identification | Independent verification and supporting evidence |
| Poor-quality images | Image-quality assessment before using results |
| Over-reliance on AI | Meaningful human oversight |
| Demographic performance differences | Regular demographic testing |
| Lack of accountability | Documentation, audits and clear responsibilities |
| Inappropriate use | Clear policies and defined purposes |
| Loss of public trust | Transparency and accountability |
| Risk to individual rights | Legal safeguards and appropriate oversight |

No single measure can eliminate every risk. Effective mitigation requires several safeguards to work together.

## 9. Recommended Approach

Based on the Robert Williams case, a responsible approach to facial recognition should follow several principles:

1. **Use facial recognition as an investigative support tool rather than definitive evidence.**
2. **Check image quality before interpreting results.**
3. **Require independent and reliable evidence before taking serious action against an individual.**
4. **Maintain meaningful human oversight throughout the decision-making process.**
5. **Test performance across relevant demographic groups.**
6. **Provide regular training for staff using the technology.**
7. **Conduct audits and document significant uses and decisions.**
8. **Ensure compliance with applicable privacy and data protection requirements.**
9. **Regularly review whether the technology is providing sufficient benefits to justify its risks.**

## 10. Conclusion

Facial recognition can provide benefits by helping organisations process and compare large collections of images efficiently. However, the Robert Williams case demonstrates that an incorrect result can have serious consequences when it is not independently verified.

The most effective approach is therefore not simply to reject the technology or rely on it without restrictions. Organisations should combine technical testing, image-quality assessment, human oversight, independent evidence, staff training, auditing and appropriate legal safeguards.

These measures can help increase the positive benefits of facial recognition while reducing the likelihood and consequences of incorrect identification.
